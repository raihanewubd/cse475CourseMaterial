K-MEANS CLUSTERING

Slide 12
  • The k-means algorithm is an algorithm to cluster n objects based on attributes into k partitions, where k < n.
  • It is similar to the expectation-maximization algorithm for mixtures of Gaussians in that they both attempt to find the centers of natural clusters in the data.
  • It assumes that the object attributes form a vector space.

K-Means

Slide 13
  • An algorithm for partitioning (or clustering) N data points into K disjoint subsets Sj containing data points so as to minimize the sum-of-squares criterion
  • where xn is a vector representing the the nth data point and uj is the geometric centroid of the data points in Sj.

K-Means

Slide 14
  • Simply speaking k-means clustering is an algorithm to classify or to group the objects based on attributes/features into K number of group.
  • K is positive integer number.
  • The grouping is done by minimizing the sum of squares of distances between data and the corresponding cluster centroid.

How the K-Mean Clustering algorithm works?

Slide 15
  • 1. Decide on a value for k.
  • 2. Initialize the k cluster centers (randomly, if necessary).
  • 3. Decide the class memberships of the N objects by assigning them to the nearest cluster center.
  • 4. Re-estimate the k cluster centers, by assuming the memberships found above are correct.
  • 5. If none of the N objects changed membership in the last iteration, exit. Otherwise goto 3.

K-means

Slide 16
  • Step 1: Begin with a decision on the value of k = number of clusters .
  • Step 2: Put any initial partition that classifies the data into k clusters. You may assign the training samples randomly, or systematically as the following:
  • Take the first k training sample as single-element clusters
  • Assign each of the remaining (N-k) training sample to the cluster with the nearest centroid. After each assignment, recompute the centroid of the gaining cluster.

K-means

Slide 17
  • Step 3: Take each sample in sequence and compute its distance from the centroid of each of the clusters. If a sample is not currently in the cluster with the closest centroid, switch this sample to that cluster and update the centroid of the cluster gaining the new sample and the cluster losing the sample.
  • Step 4 . Repeat step 3 until convergence is achieved, that is until a pass through the training sample causes no new assignments.

The K-Medoid Clustering Method

Slide 38
  • K-Medoids Clustering: Find representative objects (medoids) in clusters
  • PAM (Partitioning Around Medoids, Kaufmann & Rousseeuw 1987)
  • Starts from an initial set of medoids and iteratively replaces one of the medoids by one of the non-medoids if it improves the total distance of the resulting clustering
  • PAM works effectively for small data sets, but does not scale well for large data sets (due to the computational complexity)
  • Efficiency improvement on PAM
  • CLARA (Kaufmann & Rousseeuw, 1990): PAM on samples
  • CLARANS (Ng & Han, 1994): Randomized re-sampling

Summary

Slide 39
  • K-means and K-medoids algorithms are useful for undirected knowledge discovery and relatively simple.
  • Both of the algorithms have wide spread usage in lot of fields, ranging from pattern recognitions, image processing, machine vision, and many others.

5. DBSCAN

Density-Based Clustering: DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise)

Slide 40
  • DBSCAN is a density-based clustering algorithm that forms clusters based on the density of points.
  • It can identify clusters of arbitrary shapes and detect outliers (noise).

DBSCAN

Slide 41
  • Epsilon Neighborhood (ϵ): The region within a radius ϵ epsilon around a data point.
  • Core Point: A point with at least minPts neighbors within its ϵ -neighborhood.
  • Border Point: A point within the ϵ-neighborhood of a core point but with fewer than minPts neighbors.
  • Noise Point: A point that is neither a core point nor a border point.

DBSCAN

Slide 42
  • Mathematical Steps:

Steps of DBSCAN Algorithm:

Slide 43
  • Choose a value for eps and MinPts
  • For a particular data point (x) calculate its distance from every other datapoint.
  • Find all the neighbourhood points of x which fall inside the circle of radius (eps) or simply whose distance from x is smaller than or equal to eps.
  • Treat x as visited and if the number of neighbourhood points around x are greater or equal to MinPts then treat x as a core point and if it is not assigned to any cluster, create a new cluster and assign it to that.
  • If the number of neighbourhood points around x are less than MinPts and it has a core point in its neighbourhood, treat it as a border point.
  • Include all the density connected points as a single cluster.
  • Repeat the above steps for every unvisited point in the data set and find out all core, border and outlier points.

Direct Density Reachable vs Density Reachable

Slide 44
  • Direct density reachable: A point is called direct density reachable if it has a core point in its neighborhood.
  • Density Connected: Two points are called density connected if there is a core point which is density reachable from both the points.
  • Density Reachable: A point is called density reachable from another point if they are connected through a series of core points.

Evaluation Metrics for Clustering

Slide 79
  • External Evaluation Metrics
  • Where:
  • RI: The Rand Index, which measures the number of pairs of data points that are correctly clustered together or separated by both clustering.
  • Expected RI: The expected Rand Index under the assumption of random chance.
  • max(RI): The maximum achievable Rand Index.
  • Interpretation:
  • Higher ARI values: Indicate better agreement between the predicted clusters and the ground truth labels.
  • ARI close to 1: Suggests that the clustering algorithm has performed well in capturing the underlying structure of the data.
  • ARI close to 0: Implies that the clustering is not significantly better than random chance.
  • Negative ARI: Indicates that the clustering is worse than random chance.

Best Practices for Optimizing Clustering

Slide 80
  • Comprehensive Hyperparameter Tuning:
  • Use grid search or random search strategies over hyperparameters (e.g., ϵ -epsilon, linkage method) to identify optimal configurations.
  • Cross-Validation:
  • Apply cross-validation with different subsamples of the data to assess clustering stability and consistency across various partitions.
  • Multi-Metric Evaluation:
  • Combine multiple evaluation metrics (e.g., silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin Index) to get a holistic view of clustering performance, particularly when ground truth labels are unavailable.
  • Visual Inspection:
  • Utilize dimensionality reduction techniques (e.g., PCA, t-SNE) to visualize clusters in 2D or 3D and verify whether clusters appear well-separated and meaningful.

9.1 Silhouette score

internal · higher is better

For each point, compare how far it is from its own cluster with how far it is from the nearest other cluster. The score for the whole clustering is the mean over all points.

a(i) = mean distance from i to the other points in its own cluster
b(i) = smallest mean distance from i to the points of any other cluster
s(i) = b(i) − a(i)max(a(i), b(i))     S = 1N Σi s(i)

A point that is alone in its cluster has no a(i); by convention (Rousseeuw, 1987, and scikit-learn) its s(i) is 0.

Worked example (slides 71–72, extended to every point)

Cluster A = {1, 2, 3}, cluster B = {10, 11} on a number line. Distance is |xi − xj|.

Pointa(i)b(i)s(i)
P1 = 1 (A)(1 + 2)/2 = 1.5(9 + 10)/2 = 9.5(9.5 − 1.5)/9.5 = 0.842
P2 = 2 (A)(1 + 1)/2 = 1(8 + 9)/2 = 8.5(8.5 − 1)/8.5 = 0.882
P3 = 3 (A)(2 + 1)/2 = 1.5(7 + 8)/2 = 7.5(7.5 − 1.5)/7.5 = 0.800
P4 = 10 (B)1/1 = 1(9 + 8 + 7)/3 = 8(8 − 1)/8 = 0.875
P5 = 11 (B)1/1 = 1(10 + 9 + 8)/3 = 9(9 − 1)/9 = 0.889

S = (0.842 + 0.882 + 0.800 + 0.875 + 0.889)/5 = 0.858. The slide stops at P1 (0.84); the clustering score needs all five.

9.2 Davies–Bouldin index (DBI)

internal · lower is better

Each cluster has a scatter σ (average distance of its points to its centroid). For every pair of clusters, the ratio Rij compares their combined scatter with the distance between their centroids. Each cluster keeps its worst (largest) ratio, and DBI averages those.

σi = 1|Ci| Σx ∈ Ci d(x, μi)     Rij = σi + σjd(μi, μj)     DBI = 1K Σi=1K maxj≠i Rij

Worked example (slide 74)

C1 = {1, 2, 3}, μ1 = 2; C2 = {8, 10, 12}, μ2 = 10.

Scatter σ2(|8−10| + |10−10| + |12−10|)/3 = 4/31.333

R12 = R21(2/3 + 4/3)/8 = 2/80.25
DBI(max R1j + max R2j)/2 = (0.25 + 0.25)/20.25

With only two clusters, both rows share the same single ratio, so DBI = R12. The maxj≠i only starts to matter from K = 3; add a third cluster in the widget to see it pick the closest neighbour.

The silhouette is bounded at −1 and averages over points, so one bad point can only pull it so far. Centroid-based metrics inherit the outlier sensitivity of K-means.

9.3 Calinski–Harabasz index (CH)

internal · higher is better

Also called the variance ratio criterion. It divides the spread between cluster centroids by the spread within clusters, each scaled by its degrees of freedom. It is not on the slides; it is the third internal metric you will meet in scikit-learn.

B = Σk nk ‖μk − x̄‖²     W = Σk Σx ∈ Ck ‖x − μk‖²     CH = B / (K − 1)W / (N − K)

W is exactly the K-means objective J from tab 3. x̄ is the mean of all N points.

Worked example (same data as the DBI example)

C1 = {1, 2, 3}, C2 = {8, 10, 12}; N = 6, K = 2.

StepCalculationValue
Overall mean x̄(1 + 2 + 3 + 8 + 10 + 12)/6 = 36/66
Between B3(2 − 6)² + 3(10 − 6)² = 48 + 4896
Within W[(1−2)² + 0 + (3−2)²] + [(8−10)² + 0 + (12−10)²] = 2 + 810
CH(96 / 1) / (10 / 4) = 96 / 2.538.4

Unlike DBI and silhouette, CH has no upper limit and its size depends on N and on the units of the data. Compare CH values only across clusterings of the same dataset.

9.4 Purity

external · higher is better

For each cluster, count how many of its points belong to its most common true class. Add those counts and divide by N.

Purity = 1N Σk maxc nkc    where nkc = number of points in cluster k with true class c

9.5 Rand index (RI) and adjusted Rand index (ARI)

external · higher is better

Look at every pair of points. A pair agrees if both labelings put it together, or both put it apart. RI is the fraction of pairs that agree. ARI subtracts the agreement you would expect from a random assignment with the same cluster sizes, so a random clustering scores about 0.

RI = (pairs together in both) + (pairs apart in both)C(N, 2)

ARI = Index − ExpectedMax − Expected, with Index = Σkc C(nkc, 2), Expected = Σk C(ak, 2) · Σc C(bc, 2)C(N, 2), Max = ½[Σk C(ak, 2) + Σc C(bc, 2)]

ak = size of cluster k, bc = size of class c, C(n, 2) = n(n − 1)/2. This is the slide 79 formula (RI − Expected RI)/(max RI − Expected RI) written in pair counts; the two give the same number, with max RI = 1.

Worked example (same data)

V = {0, 0, 1, 1}, U = {0, 1, 1, 1}. There are C(4, 2) = 6 pairs.

RI = (1 + 2)/6 = 0.5.

For ARI: cluster sizes a = (1, 3), class sizes b = (2, 2), and the only cell with two or more points is (cluster 1, class 1) with 2. Index = C(2,2) = 1. Σ C(ak,2) = 0 + 3 = 3 and Σ C(bc,2) = 1 + 1 = 2. Expected = 3 × 2 / 6 = 1. Max = (3 + 2)/2 = 2.5. ARI = (1 − 1)/(2.5 − 1) = 0.

Same data, three verdicts: purity 0.75, RI 0.5, ARI 0. ARI says this clustering agrees with the truth no better than chance. The range is also narrower than slide 79 suggests: ARI cannot go below −0.5 (Chacón & Rastrojo, 2023). The "Worst case" preset reaches that floor.

9.6 Normalized mutual information (NMI)

external · higher is better

Mutual information I(U; V) measures how much knowing a point's cluster tells you about its class, in bits. Dividing by the average of the two entropies puts it on a 0 to 1 scale.

H(U) = −Σk P(k) log2 P(k)    I(U; V) = Σk,c P(k, c) log2 P(k, c)P(k) P(c)    NMI = 2 · I(U; V)H(U) + H(V)

Worked example (slides 76–78)

V = {0, 0, 1, 1}, U = {0, 1, 1, 1}.

The Unsupervised Learning Process

Slide 84
  • Model Selection:
  • Choose a suitable algorithm based on the type of unsupervised learning task (e.g., clustering, dimensionality reduction, or association).
  • Consider the nature of the data and the desired output (e.g., dense vs. sparse data, numeric vs. categorical features).
  • Model Training:
  • The algorithm processes the dataset to find patterns or clusters without any supervision. For clustering algorithms, this involves iteratively assigning data points to clusters and optimizing the grouping.
  • Model Evaluation and Interpretation:
  • In unsupervised learning, evaluating the model’s performance is more subjective since there are no ground truth labels. Metrics such as silhouette score, Davies- Bouldin index, or visual inspection through scatter plots (in lower dimensions) can be used.
  • For association rules, metrics like support, confidence, and lift are used to evaluate the significance of the rules.
  • Result Analysis:
  • Analyze the identified patterns or clusters to interpret their meaning and relevance to the problem domain. This often involves visualizing the results (e.g., cluster plots) or examining the rules generated.
