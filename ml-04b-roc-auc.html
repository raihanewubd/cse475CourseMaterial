Lecture 5 (supplement) — ROC Curves and AUC

ML-04b: the threshold sweep, the ROC curve, three ways to compute AUC, how to read one, imbalance and the precision–recall curve, the multi-class case, and where it all came from
Web notes written for CSE475, continuing Lecture 5 (Performance Evaluation). Every number was recomputed in code; blue boxes add what is usually left implicit.

In one paragraph. Lecture 5 judged a classifier from a confusion matrix, which needs hard labels. But most classifiers output a score — a probability, a logit, a distance — and the labels only appear once you pick a threshold. Pick a different threshold and you get a different confusion matrix, so a single precision or recall is a statement about one threshold, not about the model. The ROC curve removes the threshold: it sweeps every cut-off and plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate at each one. The AUC is the area under that curve, and it has an exact meaning — the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative. 1. The problem the ROC curve solves

A confusion matrix is one threshold's opinion

Continues Lecture 5

Logistic regression, a neural network, a random forest, a boosted ensemble: all of them produce a number for each input before they produce a class. Call it the score \(s(x)\). The hard label is a second step,

\[ \hat y(x)=\begin{cases}1 & s(x)\ge\tau\\ 0 & s(x)<\tau\end{cases} \]

and everything in Lecture 5 — TP, FP, FN, TN, and therefore accuracy, precision, recall, F1 — is a function of \(\tau\). Software hides this by defaulting to \(\tau=0.5\). There is nothing special about 0.5. It is the right threshold only if a false positive and a false negative cost the same and the training data had the same class balance as the deployment data, and both of those are usually false.

What goes wrong

  • Two models are compared at \(\tau=0.5\). Model A has higher F1. At \(\tau=0.3\) model B would have had higher F1. Which is better?
  • A screening test is tuned so that recall is 0.95. A reader cannot tell whether that required accepting a 10% or a 60% false positive rate.
  • A paper reports accuracy 0.97 on data that is 97% negative (the accuracy paradox from Lecture 5, tab 3).

What the ROC curve does

  • Evaluates the ranking the scores induce, over every threshold at once.
  • Shows the whole trade-off between catching positives and admitting false alarms, so the reader can choose the operating point, not the author.
  • Is unchanged by class balance (tab 5 shows exactly why, and why that is also its weakness).

The two rates

Definitions

Two of the four rates that can be formed from a confusion matrix are the axes of the ROC plot:

\[ \text{TPR}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}=\frac{TP}{P}\ (\text{recall, sensitivity, hit rate}),\qquad \text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}=\frac{FP}{N}\ (=1-\text{specificity, false-alarm rate}). \]

\(P\) and \(N\) are the numbers of actual positives and actual negatives. Each rate is normalised by its own class: TPR by the positives only, FPR by the negatives only. That is the whole reason the curve is insensitive to how many of each there are. Precision, by contrast, mixes the classes in its denominator (\(TP+FP\)), which is why it reacts to imbalance and the ROC does not.

Reading a point. A point at (FPR, TPR) = (0.2, 0.8) says: at this threshold the model catches 80% of the positives and wrongly flags 20% of the negatives. The top-left corner (0, 1) is a perfect classifier. The diagonal from (0, 0) to (1, 1) is what random guessing achieves — flag a random fraction \(q\) of everything and both rates equal \(q\). Below the diagonal the model is ranking backwards; invert its scores and it is above.

What you need before you can draw one

Prerequisites
  • Scores, not labels. A confusion matrix cannot be turned into an ROC curve: it has already thrown the ranking away. In scikit-learn use predict_proba or decision_function, not predict. In PyTorch keep the logits or softmax outputs.
  • A binary question. ROC is defined for positive-vs-negative. With \(K\) classes you ask \(K\) binary questions and average (tab 6).
  • A test set the model has not seen. Same rule as every metric in Lecture 5.

The scores do not need to be probabilities, and they do not need to be calibrated. Any monotone transformation — logit to probability, probability to percentile — leaves the ranking alone and so leaves the curve and the AUC alone. That is a strength (you can compare an SVM margin with a logistic probability) and a blind spot (a model that says 0.99 for everything positive and 0.98 for everything negative gets AUC = 1 and would be useless as a probability estimate).

The threshold sweep

Step by step

Start with \(\tau\) above every score, so nothing is predicted positive: TP = FP = 0, the point (0, 0). Then lower \(\tau\) to each score in turn. 3. The area under the curve, computed three ways

Way 1 — geometry: add up the rectangles

Definition

With no tied scores the curve is a staircase, so the area under it is a sum of rectangles. Each horizontal step (a negative admitted) has width \(1/N=0.2\) and height equal to the TPR reached so far. Reading the horizontal steps off the sweep table in tab 2:

Negative admittedTPR at that momentWidthRectangle

E0.60.20.12
G0.80.20.16

For real-valued scores with ties, software uses the trapezoidal rule between consecutive points, \(\sum_k (x_{k+1}-x_k)\,\tfrac{y_k+y_{k+1}}{2}\), which reduces to the rectangles above when every step is purely horizontal or purely vertical. sklearn.metrics.auc(fpr, tpr) is exactly that sum.

Way 2 — probability: count the correctly ordered pairs

Hanley & McNeil (1982)

Take every (positive, negative) pair — there are \(P\times N=25\) of them — and ask whether the positive is scored higher. The AUC is the fraction of pairs where it is:

\[ \text{AUC}=\frac{1}{PN}\sum_{i\in\text{pos}}\ \sum_{j\in\text{neg}} \mathbb{1}\!\left[s_i>s_j\right]+\tfrac12\,\mathbb{1}\!\left[s_i=s_j\right]. \]
PositiveScoreNegatives scored below itCount
A0.95C, E, G, I, J5
B0.90C, E, G, I, J5
D0.80E, G, I, J (loses to C)4
F0.65G, I, J (loses to C, E)3
H0.45I, J (loses to C, E, G)2
Correctly ordered pairs19 of 25
\[ \text{AUC}=\frac{19}{25}=0.76 . \]

This is the interpretation to remember. AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative. It says nothing about any particular threshold, and nothing about the numerical values of the scores — only their order. The six mis-ordered pairs (C above D, F, H; E above F, H; G above H) are exactly the six "missing" cells in the staircase, each worth \(1/25=0.04\): \(1-6\times0.04=0.76\).

Way 3 — ranks: the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney statistic

Statistics link

Counting pairs is \(O(PN)\). Sorting is faster and gives the same number. Rank all \(P+N\) scores from 1 (lowest) to \(P+N\) (highest), with tied scores sharing the average rank, and let \(R_+\) be the sum of the ranks of the positives. Then

\[ \text{AUC}=\frac{R_+-\tfrac12P(P+1)}{PN}=\frac{U}{PN}, \]

where \(U\) is the Mann–Whitney U statistic of the two-sample test. Here the ranks are J=1, I=2, H=3, G=4, F=5, E=6, D=7, C=8, B=9, A=10, so \(R_+=3+5+7+9+10=34\) and

\[ \text{AUC}=\frac{34-\tfrac12\cdot5\cdot6}{25}=\frac{34-15}{25}=\frac{19}{25}=0.76 . \]

The subtraction \(\tfrac12P(P+1)\) removes the ranks the positives would have earned by being compared with each other; what is left counts positives-above-negatives only. This identity is why "AUC" and "Mann–Whitney U" are the same test in disguise, and why a statistical package can give you a \(p\)-value for AUC = 0.5 for free (tab 4).

Why the three agree. In the staircase, each horizontal step belongs to one negative \(j\) and its height is (number of positives already admitted)/\(P\) — which is exactly the number of positives scored above \(j\). Summing \(\tfrac1N\times\tfrac{\#\{i:\,s_i>s_j\}}{P}\) over the negatives is the pair count divided by \(PN\). Way 3 is way 2 computed through a sort.

4. Reading an AUC — what 0.76 does and does not tell you

Against the baseline, then against a rule of thumb

Interpretation

The first comparison is with 0.5, not with 0. An AUC of 0.76 means the model orders 76% of positive–negative pairs correctly where coin-flipping would order 50%. The widely quoted bands below come from Hosmer and Lemeshow's logistic-regression textbook; they are a convention from clinical risk modelling, and what counts as "good" is domain-specific — an AUC of 0.75 is strong for predicting hospital readmission and weak for reading a digit.

0.50chance
0.5 – 0.7poor
0.7 – 0.8acceptable
0.8 – 0.9excellent
0.9 – 1.0outstanding

Hosmer & Lemeshow (2000, ch. 5). Our example, 0.76, sits in "acceptable". Treat the labels as vocabulary, not as a verdict.

Same AUC, different model

What the number hides

Two models can have identical AUC and be useful in opposite situations. Model P ranks three positives at the very top but puts one positive dead last. Model Q puts one negative at the very top and then ranks everything else well. Both score 19 of 25 pairs correctly.

RequirementModel PModel Q
Zero false alarms allowed (FPR = 0)catches 60% of positivescatches 0%
Must catch every positive (TPR = 1)needs FPR = 1.0 (flag everything)needs FPR = 0.4
AUC0.760.76

A fraud team that can investigate only a handful of cases wants P. A cancer screen that must not miss a case wants Q. The AUC averages over the whole FPR range with equal weight, and most applications care about one end of it. When the operating region is known, report the partial AUC over that FPR range, or simply the TPR at the FPR you can afford.

Choosing the threshold: the curve shows the options, the costs choose

Operating point

A common rule is to pick the point farthest above the diagonal, i.e. maximise Youden's J \(=\text{TPR}-\text{FPR}\). On the sweep table of tab 2, J = 0.4 at four different thresholds: \(\tau=0.90\) (0.4 − 0), 0.80 (0.6 − 0.2), 0.65 (0.8 − 0.4) and 0.45 (1.0 − 0.6). The ROC curve cannot break the tie because it does not know what a mistake costs. Attach costs and it can:

Cost = \(c_{FN}\cdot FN + c_{FP}\cdot FP\). Fawcett (2006) gives the general rule: the optimal point is where the curve touches a line of slope \(\dfrac{c_{FP}\,N}{c_{FN}\,P}\); the two columns above are slopes 1/5 and 5. Note that the right threshold depends on the deployment class ratio \(N/P\), which is one more reason not to trust the default 0.5.

How sure is 0.76? Standard error and comparing two AUCs

Hanley & McNeil; DeLong

An AUC from ten instances is an estimate from a sample of 25 pairs, and it deserves an error bar. Hanley and McNeil (1982) give a closed form:

\[ \text{SE}(A)=\sqrt{\frac{A(1-A)+(P-1)(Q_1-A^2)+(N-1)(Q_2-A^2)}{PN}},\qquad Q_1=\frac{A}{2-A},\quad Q_2=\frac{2A^2}{1+A}. \]

For \(A=0.76,\ P=N=5\): \(Q_1=0.6129\), \(Q_2=0.6564\), and

\[ \text{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{0.1824+4(0.6129-0.5776)+4(0.6564-0.5776)}{25}}=\sqrt{\frac{0.1824+0.1413+0.3151}{25}}=0.160 . \]

A 95% interval of \(0.76\pm1.96\times0.160\) runs from 0.45 to 1.0 (clipped). The example's AUC is not distinguishable from chance: the exact Mann–Whitney test for these ten scores gives \(p=0.22\). The same 0.76 would have SE 0.048 with 50 instances per class and 0.015 with 500. Any AUC quoted to three decimals from a test set of a few dozen cases is noise dressed as precision.

Two models on the same test set. Their AUCs are correlated (same instances), so a two-sample test on the two SEs is wrong. DeLong, DeLong & Clarke-Pearson (1988) give the correct nonparametric test; it is implemented in R's pROC::roc.test and in several Python packages. For a course project, a bootstrap over test instances — resample, recompute both AUCs, look at the distribution of the difference — is simpler and makes the same point. Report the interval, not just the delta.

Three more things AUC does not measure

Limits
  • Calibration. Scores of (0.99 positive, 0.98 negative) and (0.99, 0.01) both give AUC = 1. If the probabilities will be used as probabilities — expected cost, risk communication — check a reliability diagram or the Brier score as well.
  • Precision. AUC never looks at \(TP/(TP+FP)\). On imbalanced data an AUC of 0.9 can coexist with a precision of 0.1 at every useful threshold (tab 5).
  • A consistent cost model. Hand (2009) showed that AUC implicitly weights FPR–TPR trade-offs with a distribution that depends on the classifier's own score distribution, so comparing two models by AUC uses different cost weightings for each. His proposed replacement (the H-measure) is rarely used, but the criticism is a reason to prefer a stated operating point whenever one is known.

5. Imbalance: why the ROC flatters, and the precision–recall curve

Multiply the negatives by twenty — nothing happens to the ROC

Experiment

Take the ten instances of tab 2 and add nineteen more copies of each negative, so the test set is 5 positives against 100 negatives (prevalence 4.8%). At every threshold the FP count is twenty times larger, and so is \(N\); the ratio \(FP/N\) is unchanged. TPR never involved the negatives at all. So every point of the curve stays where it was and the AUC is still 0.76.

Now look at the threshold \(\tau=0.80\), which in the balanced set gave 3 TP and 1 FP, precision 0.75. In the imbalanced set it gives 3 TP and 20 FP: precision 3/23 = 0.13. Eighty-seven percent of the alarms are false, and the ROC curve has not moved.

This is not a flaw in the definition. ROC answers "how well does the model separate the two score distributions?", and that question has the same answer whatever the mix. It becomes misleading only when the reader takes a high AUC to mean the alarms can be trusted. On rare-positive problems — fraud, disease screening, defect detection — that is usually the question being asked, so the ROC is answering the wrong one.

The precision–recall curve and average precision

Definition and worked example

Same sweep, different axes: recall (= TPR) on the x-axis, precision on the y-axis. Because precision is not monotone in the threshold, the curve is a sawtooth, dropping each time a negative is admitted and recovering as positives follow. The usual summary is average precision (AP): the precision at each positive's rank, averaged over the positives, which is the area under the step-wise curve.

Positive admittedTP so farFP so farRecallPrecision at that point
A100.21/1 = 1.000
B200.42/2 = 1.000
D310.63/4 = 0.750
F420.84/6 = 0.667

Average precision = mean of the five(1 + 1 + 0.75 + 0.667 + 0.625)/5 = 0.808

With the negatives multiplied by twenty the same computation gives AP = 0.46, against an ROC-AUC still at 0.76. The random baseline also moves: a classifier that scores at random has precision equal to the prevalence everywhere, so the PR baseline is 0.5 on the balanced set and 0.048 on the imbalanced one, whereas the ROC baseline is 0.5 in both. A PR-AUC must always be read against its own prevalence.

Two technical points (Davis & Goadrich, 2006). (1) One curve dominates another in ROC space if and only if it dominates in PR space, so the curves never disagree about a clear winner — they disagree about how big the gap looks and where it is. (2) Straight-line interpolation between PR points is wrong (precision is a ratio whose denominator changes along the curve); sklearn.metrics.average_precision_score uses the step-wise sum above, deliberately, and the older trapezoidal auc(recall, precision) is optimistic.

The project rubric for this course grades on macro-F1, which is a threshold-dependent, prevalence-sensitive metric. ROC-AUC is a useful additional number there, chiefly to show that a low F1 comes from a bad threshold rather than a bad ranking — or the reverse.

6. More than two classes

One-vs-rest: K binary questions

Definition

ROC needs a positive class and a negative class. With \(K\) classes the standard move is one-vs-rest (OvR): for class \(c\), call the instances of class \(c\) positive, everything else negative, and use the model's score for class \(c\) — the \(c\)-th softmax output — as the binary score. That gives \(K\) ROC curves and \(K\) AUCs, which are then averaged in one of three ways:

\[ \text{macro}=\frac1K\sum_c \text{AUC}_c,\qquad \text{weighted}=\sum_c \frac{n_c}{n}\,\text{AUC}_c,\qquad \text{micro}=\text{AUC of the pooled }(nK)\text{ binary decisions}. \]

The alternative, one-vs-one (OvO), computes an AUC for each of the \(\binom K2\) class pairs using only those two classes' instances and averages them (Hand & Till, 2001). It ignores how the model confuses each pair with the other classes, which makes it less sensitive to imbalance; scikit-learn offers both.

The course project rubric uses macro for F1 for exactly this reason: it refuses to let the majority class hide the minority.

What multi-class AUC does not fix

Caveats
  • OvR AUC can be high while accuracy is mediocre (0.90 vs 0.75 above). The two measure different things: ranking within each class's score column versus the argmax across columns. Report both.
  • Each OvR question is itself imbalanced (one class against \(K-1\)), so every point of tab 5 applies: with ten classes, each binary question is at best 10:90, and a per-class AUC of 0.95 says little about per-class precision.
  • Micro-averaged AUC is sometimes used because it is "one number from one curve", but on imbalanced data it reduces to the majority class's AUC and should not be the headline.

7. Where the ROC curve came from

Radar, then psychology, then medicine, then machine learning

Timeline

The name is the giveaway. A "receiver" is a radio receiver; its "operating characteristic" is how its output behaves as the operator turns the sensitivity knob. During the Second World War, radar operators had to decide whether a blip on the screen was an aircraft or noise. Turn the gain up and you catch more aircraft and more noise; turn it down and you miss both. Engineers plotted the two rates against each other as the gain varied, and the resulting curve described the receiver, independent of the setting chosen on any particular night.

1954 — Peterson, Birdsall & Fox, "The theory of signal detectability"
The curve becomes mathematics: the optimal detector is a likelihood-ratio test, and sweeping its threshold traces an ROC curve that is concave and lies above the chance line. The intuition that a curve characterises a detector and a point characterises a decision dates from here.
1966 — Green & Swets, Signal Detection Theory and Psychophysics
The same mathematics applied to people: a subject deciding whether a faint tone was present behaves like a receiver with a threshold. Sensitivity (\(d'\)) and bias are separated, which is the psychological version of "ranking versus threshold".
1982 — Hanley & McNeil, "The meaning and use of the area under a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve", Radiology
Radiologists were comparing diagnostic tests by AUC without a clear reading of what the area meant. This paper proved AUC equals the probability that a random diseased case is rated higher than a random healthy one, identified it with the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney statistic, and gave the standard-error formula in tab 4.
1988 — DeLong, DeLong & Clarke-Pearson, Biometrics
The test for comparing two AUCs measured on the same patients, still the default in medical statistics.
1989 — Spackman; 1997 — Bradley, Pattern Recognition
ROC analysis crosses into machine learning. Bradley's paper argued for AUC as the standard single-number comparison of classifiers, on the grounds that accuracy depends on the threshold and the class ratio and AUC does not.
2006 — Fawcett, "An introduction to ROC analysis", Pattern Recognition Letters
The tutorial most ML papers cite: the efficient one-pass algorithm for the curve, the convex hull, iso-cost lines for choosing an operating point, averaging curves across folds, and the multi-class extension. If you read one paper from this tab, read this one.
2006 — Davis & Goadrich; 2015 — Saito & Rehmsmeier
The pushback: on imbalanced data the ROC gives an optimistic picture, and the precision–recall curve is the honest one. These two are the basis of tab 5.
Why the history matters for reading papers. The three communities use different words for the same objects. Medicine says sensitivity and specificity (1 − FPR), psychology says hit rate and false-alarm rate, machine learning says recall and FPR; "AUC" in a medical paper usually means ROC-AUC, while in an information-retrieval paper "AP" or "mAP" is the PR-curve area. Knowing which community a paper comes from tells you which curve its numbers describe.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import roc_curve, roc_auc_score, auc, average_precision_score

score = np.array([0.95, 0.90, 0.85, 0.80, 0.70, 0.65, 0.55, 0.45, 0.30, 0.20])
y     = np.array([1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0])

fpr, tpr, thresholds = roc_curve(y, score)   # the sweep of tab 2
print(np.c_[thresholds, fpr, tpr])
print(auc(fpr, tpr))                           # 0.76  (trapezoid, way 1)
print(roc_auc_score(y, score))                 # 0.76  (same thing, one call)
print(average_precision_score(y, score))       # 0.8083 (tab 5)

# way 2 by hand: fraction of correctly ordered (positive, negative) pairs
pos, neg = score[y == 1], score[y == 0]
pairs = (pos[:, None] > neg[None, :]) + 0.5 * (pos[:, None] == neg[None, :])
print(pairs.mean())                            # 0.76

# way 3 by hand: Mann-Whitney through ranks
from scipy.stats import rankdata
r = rankdata(score)                            # average ranks for ties
P, N = len(pos), len(neg)
print((r[y == 1].sum() - P * (P + 1) / 2) / (P * N))   # 0.76
Gotchas. roc_curve prepends a threshold of inf so the curve starts at (0, 0); it also drops collinear points by default (drop_intermediate=True), so the number of points can be smaller than the number of distinct scores. Always pass scores: roc_auc_score(y, clf.predict(X)) silently computes the AUC of a hard 0/1 prediction, which is just \(\tfrac12(\text{TPR}+\text{TNR})\) — balanced accuracy — and is not what you meant. Use clf.predict_proba(X)[:, 1] or clf.decision_function(X).

Key takeaways

Summary
ROC curveTPR against FPR as the threshold sweeps from \(+\infty\) down. Built from scores, not labels. A staircase for untied scores: up for each positive admitted, right for each negative. AUCThe area under it, equal to \(\Pr(s_{\text{pos}}>s_{\text{neg}})\), equal to \(U/(PN)\). Chance 0.5, perfect 1. Rank-based, so threshold- and prevalence-free, and blind to calibration and precision. Reading oneCompare with 0.5, then with the domain. Same AUC can mean different curves; state the operating region. Attach a standard error — with ten instances, 0.76 is not distinguishable from 0.5. ThresholdThe curve shows the options; the costs \(c_{FN},c_{FP}\) and the deployment class ratio choose. Youden's J is a cost-free default and will tie. ImbalanceROC does not move when negatives multiply; precision does. For rare positives report PR-AUC / AP against a baseline equal to the prevalence. Multi-classOne-vs-rest curves averaged macro, weighted or micro; say which. Macro protects the minority class.

Practice questions

Try these
  1. A model scores eight instances; sorted from highest score, the true classes are + + − + − − + −. Draw the ROC curve, compute the AUC by rectangles and by pair counting, and check the two agree.
  2. Repeat question 1 with the ranks: compute \(R_+\) and verify \((R_+-\tfrac12P(P+1))/(PN)\) gives the same AUC.
  3. Two instances in question 1 — the third and fourth — are now given the same score. Recompute the AUC. Which step of the staircase became diagonal?
  4. A classifier has AUC 0.30. Is it useless? What single change makes it a 0.70 classifier, and why does that change not help a classifier with AUC 0.50?
  5. From the sweep table in tab 2, find every threshold at which Youden's J is maximal. A false negative costs 3 and a false positive costs 1: which of them minimises total cost? What if the deployment data has 20 negatives for every positive?
  6. Using the Hanley–McNeil formula, compute the standard error of AUC = 0.90 with \(P=N=20\). Does a second model with AUC = 0.85 on the same 40 cases differ "significantly"? Explain why the question as posed cannot be answered with the two SEs alone.
  7. Explain in two sentences why multiplying the number of negatives by 20 leaves the ROC curve unchanged but moves the PR curve. Which denominator changed?
  8. A fraud model has ROC-AUC 0.95 and average precision 0.12 on data with 1% fraud. Describe what the alarms look like to the analyst who receives them.
  9. In the twelve-row table of tab 6, which single instance would you change, and how, to raise the Dog one-vs-rest AUC by the largest amount? Recompute it.
  10. Write a function that computes AUC from scores and labels in \(O(n\log n)\) time without calling a library, and verify it on the tab-2 data.
